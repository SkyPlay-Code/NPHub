Q&A: The Right Stuff

Ever wonder why Uranium-235 and Plutonium-239 are the celebrities of the nuclear world? Let's explore what makes an element "fissile" and right for the job.

Why is Uranium-235 so special?

Uranium-235 is the star player in devices like "Little Boy" for a few key reasons. The same logic generally applies to Plutonium-239, used in "Fat Man."

  • It's Fissile: This is the most important trait. "Fissile" means that when a U-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron, it's very likely to split apart (fission), releasing a huge amount of energy and, critically, more neutrons. These new neutrons can then go on to split other U-235 atoms, creating a self-sustaining chain reaction.
  • Manageable Critical Mass: To get an explosion, you need a "critical mass"—the minimum amount of material needed for a chain reaction to take off. U-235 has a critical mass that is small enough to fit inside a deliverable bomb.
  • It's Available (Barely): While natural uranium is mostly U-238 (about 99.3%), a tiny fraction (0.7%) is U-235. It takes a massive industrial effort to enrich uranium and isolate enough U-235, but it's possible because it exists in nature. To put its power in perspective, 1 kg of enriched uranium can release the same energy as millions of kilograms of coal.

So, why can't we use something common, like Thorium?

This is a great question. Thorium is much more abundant than uranium, but it has one major drawback: it's not fissile.

A Thorium-232 nucleus will happily absorb a neutron, but it won't split. Instead, it becomes unstable and eventually decays into a new isotope, Uranium-233. This new U-233 *is* fissile, much like U-235. Elements like Thorium that can be turned into fissile material are called "fertile."

While this process is extremely useful for nuclear reactors (where you can breed new fuel), it makes Thorium useless for a bomb. A bomb needs an instant chain reaction, not a slow conversion process that requires a reactor to even get started. On top of that, the process often creates U-232 as a contaminant, which is intensely radioactive and makes handling the material extremely difficult and dangerous.

In short, for a bomb, you need a material that's ready to go right out of the box. That's why U-235 and Pu-239 are the elements of choice.
